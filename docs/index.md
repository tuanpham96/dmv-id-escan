# _Holup, did you just scan my ID?_

## The ask

To understand whether ID scanning is widespread across DMV local businesses, I've created a short survey to crowd-source the businesses, specifically those in the service industry (e.g., bars, liquor stores) in DMV, that electronically scan people's ID cards to provide restricted services. This is, instead of just looking at the card to verify age or identity, a machine such as a portable hand-held scanner is involved.

## Motivations

### The observations

Normally, when you buy liquor or go in a bar, they just look at your ID to verify your age. But have there been times where they scan your ID card with some machines? Or maybe sometimes you're just too occupied to notice, then realize it only afterwards.

Lately, I think I've experienced this myself, or heard from others or read online that businesses in the DMV area scanning their customers' IDs. I only started to notice this after reading a [piece by EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/07/why-are-gay-bars-building-databases-their-patrons) (Electronic Frontier Foundation) in the summer of 2026. Specifically, gay bars in San Francisco reportedly used PatronScan to scan IDs and even facces; fortunately, some bars stopped using it. 

### Why is it problematic?

For the case of gay bars as an example, it is already worrisome as sensitive information of those in the LGBTQ community may be stored and shared with who-knows-whom-else. One of the reasons why businesses do this is to identify and share with one another information about potential troublesome individuals. But in essence, consumers are likely trading away their privacy to attain such services. Those in vulnerable communities could be subject under such privacy-invading practices when they go out just to have fun. It is especially concerning under the current political climate, as well as various reports of data breaches and corporate surveillance of Big Tech. 

The barcode of your ID card contains private and sensitive information, such as name, date of birth, and address. The difference between a physical verification (i.e., just looking) and electronic scanning is that all this information comes in a structured format and can be stored, transfered, and shared with other parties. The person selling you a beer can choose to just look at your birth year and then forget, but electronic methods record not only your information but your association with such purchases, then allow sharing with additional parties, likely without your consent or knowledge.

Some of such information is certainly already available elsewhere, such as your bank. And such verification method is justified in places such as government offices and airports. But should we be OK with our data being increasingly collected at the expense of supposed security of businesses, especially if we are not informed or offered a chance to opt-out or use alternative methods? 

### Any legal protections?

In the San Francisco case, there are legal arguments to be made to restrict, and even ban, this type of practices in California (see the above EFF article). It is unclear to me how much consumer protection is available for those residing or visting DMV areas. 

It may also be tricky as the DMV area may be subject to three different jurisdictions. And even if there's some privacy or data protection law, we also have to be worried about the extent to which businesses and those third-party scanning services actually comply with the requirements set out by the relevant laws. 

If there is anyone with legal expertise in the matter, I'd love to know more; my email is `tuanhpham[AT]pm[DOT]me`. In the mean time, I want to know how egregious ID scanning is in the area, and hopefully such information could be useful for someone. At least we can have some sense of how serious it is, or maybe not at all.
